Tenant Rights

The right to organize

New York law protects tenants who organize together to defend their rights. Landlords cannot obstruct lawful tenant organizing, punish participants, or take away tenancy rights or benefits because they organize.

Tenant groups may hold peaceful meetings at reasonable times in shared areas of the property without paying a fee, provided access remains unobstructed.

Safe homes and essential repairs

Tenants are entitled to safe, well-maintained homes. Owners are responsible for required repairs and essential services, including heat and hot water, and for addressing hazards such as leaks, mold, pests, and unsafe building conditions.

Keep dated photos, written repair requests, and complaint numbers. Notify ownership about problems; if they remain unresolved, report them through 311. HPD can inspect conditions and issue violations. Tenants can also bring an HP proceeding in Housing Court to ask for an order requiring repairs and restoration of services.

The Loft Law and rent stabilization

New York’s Loft Law protects qualifying residents of former commercial and manufacturing spaces and requires owners to legalize covered homes. Protected occupants receive rent protections during legalization and, once the covered units are legalized, must be offered rent-regulated leases. Coverage depends on statutory occupancy periods, residential Certificate of Occupancy history, and other eligibility requirements; the Loft Board decides coverage applications.

An apartment described as “market rate” may already be legally subject to rent stabilization. Tenants can request their rent history and investigate whether an exemption or deregulation was lawful, including claims of substantial rehabilitation. Past court and agency decisions affect which challenges remain available.

Some tax-benefit and affordable-housing programs also bring previously unregulated apartments into rent stabilization. The protections and their duration depend on the program. A building’s records and legal history determine which route applies.

Challenging neglect through Article 7A

When qualifying dangerous conditions persist in a building, a group of at least one-third of its tenants can ask Housing Court to appoint an Article 7A administrator in place of the owner as active manager.

The administrator collects rents and uses the money to restore essential services and make necessary repairs under court supervision. The owner retains legal title, while tenants pay rent to the administrator as directed by the court. This gives organized tenants a way to seek a change in management when serious neglect threatens their homes.

From tenants to collective owners

Tenants can organize to purchase their building from the landlord and create a resident-controlled cooperative. In an affordable HDFC cooperative, residents own shares in the corporation that owns the building and elect its board. Income and resale restrictions help preserve affordability for future residents.

For eligible distressed properties, city tax-foreclosure and transfer processes can also create a route toward tenant ownership. These require government action and program approval. HPD’s Affordable Neighborhood Cooperative Program works with qualifying city-owned buildings to rehabilitate them and transfer them to resident-controlled cooperatives.

A transfer of ownership requires a negotiated sale or an authorized legal process, together with a workable acquisition and repair plan. UHAB helps tenant groups explore purchase strategies and the steps toward cooperative ownership.
